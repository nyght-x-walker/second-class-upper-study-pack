• Weather is the combination of temperature, humidity,
  precipitation, wind, cloudiness, and other atmospheric
  conditions occurring at a specific place and time.

• Climate is the long-term average pattern of weather and may
  be local, regional or global.

• The nature of terrestrial ecosystems and their dominant plants
  is a reflection of prevailing climatic conditions.

  wavelengths depending on the
  temperature of the object.

• The hotter the object the shorter the

• A very hot surface such as that of the Sun (~5800° C) gives
  off primarily shortwave radiation.

• In contrast, cooler objects such as Earth’s surface (average
  temperature of 15°C) emit radiation of longer wavelengths
  or longwave radiation.

• Of the solar radiation that reaches the top of Earth’s
  atmosphere, only 51 percent makes it to the surface.

Unit 1: The physical environment
 What happens to all the incoming energy?
• Assuming 100 units of solar radiation from the sun reaches
  the earth’s outer surface.

• The clouds and atmosphere would reflect and scatter 26
  units. The earths surface reflects 4 units. Thus, a total of 30
  units is reflected into space.

• The atmosphere and clouds in addition absorb 19 units. The
  remaining 51 units reach the earth’s surface.



    11/2/2026                        B. Ofosu-Bamfo                 11
Unit 1: The physical environment
• 23 units of the 51 units that reaches the earth surface are
  used to evaporate water and 7 units are used to warm air.

• The remaining 21 units are absorbed by the landmasses and
  oceans.

• Meanwhile, landmasses and oceans themselves emit
  longwave radiations that exceed 21 units, up to the tune of
  about 117 units. Only about 6 units of this radiation escapes
  into space.



    11/2/2026                      B. Ofosu-Bamfo                 12
Unit 1: The physical environment
• The remaining (111 units) is absorbed by the clouds, water
  vapour and CO2 in the atmosphere.

• Much of this energy (96 units) absorbed by the clouds is
  emitted back to the earth. This is what creates the
  greenhouse effect.

• In effect, the energy lost at Earth’s surface (30 units + 117
  units = 147 units) is exactly balanced by the energy gained
  (51 units + 96 units = 147 units). The radiation budget of
  Earth is in balance.

Air mass circulation
• The atmosphere represents a blanket of air that surrounds the
  earth. This blanket of air is not static.
• It’s movement is driven by rising and sinking air masses and
  the rotation of earth on it’s axis.

• Warm air rises because it is less dense. In the equatorial
  region, air is heated and it rises to the top of the troposphere
  (zone of low pressure).
• Further warming and rising of air beneath this zone forces the
  rising air mass to spread northward and southward to the
  poles.

• As this air moves towards the poles, they become cool
  and dense and begin to sink. Sinking air raises air pressure
  (high pressure zone).

• The cool heavier air is thus driven to replace the warm air

• Air mass circulation from the equator to the poles is
  influenced by the Coriolis effect.

• It creates a series of belts of prevailing winds, named for
  the direction they come from.

• Air heated in the equatorial zone rises upward, creating a
  low-pressure zone near the surface. This upward flow of
  air is balanced by a flow of air from the north and south
  toward the equator.



   11/2/2026                      B. Ofosu-Bamfo                27

• As the warm air mass rises it begins to spread, diverging
  northward and southward toward the North and South Poles,
  cooling as it goes.

• In the Northern Hemisphere, the Coriolis effect forces air in
  an easterly direction, slowing its progress north. At about
  30° north latitude, the now cool air sinks.

• The descending air forms a semipermanent high-pressure
  belt at the surface and encircling Earth. This air cools, splits
  into two currents, one flowing northward and the other
  returning to the equator as northeast trade winds. Similar but
  opposite flow in the southern hemisphere creates the
  southeast trade winds.

 Soil formation
• Weathering of rocks (mechanical destruction and chemical
  modification).
• Mechanical weathering results from the interaction of
  several forces. When exposed to the combined action of
  water, wind, and temperature, rock surfaces flake and peel
  away.
• Water seeps into crevices, freezes, expands, and cracks the
  rock into smaller pieces.
• Wind-borne particles, such as dust and sand, wear away at
  the rock surface. Growing roots of trees split rock apart.


   11/2/2026                      B. Ofosu-Bamfo                43
Unit 1: The physical environment
• Chemical weathering happens due to the presence of water,
  oxygen, and acids resulting from the activities of soil
  organisms and the continual addition of organic matter (dead
  plant and animal tissues).
• Rainwater falling on and filtering through this organic
  matter and mineral soil sets up a chain of chemical reactions
  that transform the composition of the original rocks and
  minerals.

• Five interdependent factors are important in soil formation:
  parent material, climate, biotic factors, topography, and time.

 Moisture holding capacity of soil
• Water moves into soils by infiltration. The size of soil pore
  spaces and soil particles determine the levels of infiltration.

• Soil is saturated when there is more water than the pore
  space can hold. Soil is said to be at field capacity (FC) when
  water in pore spaces is held by internal capillary forces.

• Water held by capillary forces is known as capillary water.
  Soil is at wilting point (WP) when its water content declines
  beyond the reach of plants.

• Available water capacity = FC-WP

• Cation exchange capacity (CEC) refers to the total number of
